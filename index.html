📐 How this recommendation works — the decision logic

Fit score (0–100%) is calculated from two inputs you set in Steps 1 and 2:

• Urban core population — larger cities justify higher-capacity, higher-cost modes

• Suburb population — large suburban rings push the score toward commuter rail and BRT feeders

• Combined (urban + suburb) is the primary driver — each mode has a population threshold range where it performs best

Score thresholds:
⭐ Best ≥ 80% — strong population match, mode is cost-justified
✅ Good 60–79% — viable, may need careful corridor selection
⚡ OK 35–59% — possible but likely oversized or undersized
✗ Poor < 35% — not cost-effective at your city scale

Comparison table shows four additional dimensions for each mode:

• Daily riders — estimated at 35% of peak hourly capacity × operating hours, scaled by line length. Not a demand forecast — a supply ceiling.

• Build cost — capital expenditure based on cost-per-km × default network length for your city size and terrain

• Build time — weighted average across all construction phases (planning → civil works → commissioning)

• 30-yr value (NPV) — fare income minus operating cost minus capex, discounted at 4%. Negative is normal for public transit — the social and economic benefit is not captured here.

⚠️ Important: This is a first-order screening tool. It ranks modes by population fit and rough cost — it does not replace a full feasibility study, demand model, or corridor analysis. Use it to shortlist 1–2 modes, then go deeper in Steps 4–5.

👥 What this chart shows

Each coloured line shows how many passengers per day are predicted to use that transit option over 30 years. The lines usually start low and rise — this is the ramp-up period, when people are still discovering the new service and changing their travel habits.

Why does ridership grow slowly at first? New transit services typically take 3–8 years to reach full ridership. People need time to move closer to stations, employers need time to relocate, and developers need time to build housing near the route. This is called Transit-Oriented Development (TOD).

What to look for: A scenario with more passengers at year 30 serves more people — but also look at how quickly it reaches high ridership. A fast ramp-up means quicker revenue and less time needing public subsidy.

International benchmark: A new tram line in a medium-sized city typically reaches 20,000–60,000 passengers/day at maturity. A metro in a large city: 100,000–400,000/day.

Key financial figures compared
Build cost, annual revenue, running costs and 30-year value
ℹ️
💰 What this chart shows

The grouped bars compare four numbers side by side for each scenario:

  • Build cost — how much it costs to construct (tunnels, tracks, stations, vehicles). This is a one-time upfront expense, usually spread over 5–15 years of construction.
  • Income per year — annual ticket revenue once the system is running at full capacity. Higher is better — it reduces the ongoing public subsidy needed.
  • Running costs per year — the annual cost to operate the system (drivers, maintenance, energy, administration). Every transit system has these ongoing costs forever.
  • 30-year value (NPV ÷ 10) — the Net Present Value over 30 years, scaled down for readability. A positive bar means the system earns back more than it costs over its lifetime. A negative bar means it requires permanent public subsidy — which may still be justified for social or environmental reasons.

What is NPV in plain English? It answers: "If we invest €1 billion today, how much is the total stream of future revenues worth in today's money?" We discount future money because €1 received in 20 years is worth less than €1 today (inflation, opportunity cost). An NPV above zero means the investment pays for itself.

Tip: A scenario with a lower build cost but similar income is almost always preferable — the gap is money that can be spent on other public services.

📐 How fit scores and value-for-money work in the complex simulation
📊 Fit score (left chart)

Each mode has a population sweet spot — the combined urban + suburb population range where it delivers best value.

The score (0–100%) measures how close your city's population is to that sweet spot:

• Metro: optimal above 1.5M — tunnelling cost is only justified at high ridership

• Tram: peaks at 150k–900k — the standard backbone for mid-size cities

• BRT: strong at 60k–400k — ~80% of tram quality at ~20% of cost

• eBus / Hybrid / Biogas: score well below 200k — best as local/feeder modes at any city size

💰 Value for money (right chart)

The scatter chart plots each mode by build cost per km (x-axis) vs daily passengers per km (y-axis). Bubble size = total capex.

Bottom-left zone — cheap and lower capacity: BRT, bus modes. Fast to build, low cost, good feeder value.

Top-right zone — expensive and high capacity: Metro, LRT. High capital cost justified only if demand is high.

Ideal combination: one mode from each zone — a high-capacity trunk covering the main corridor, and a lower-cost mode distributing passengers to/from it.

🧭 Decision guide

< 200k people: Start with eBus or BRT. Tram on one main street if budget allows. No metro.

200k – 800k: Tram or LRT as backbone. BRT or trolleybus as feeders. Commuter rail if suburbs are 15km+.

800k – 2M: Metro on 1–2 key corridors + tram/LRT distributors + BRT feeders. Classic three-tier network.

> 2M: Full metro network (4–6 lines) + commuter rail + surface distributors. Simultaneous construction risk is high — phase carefully.

⚠️ Any size: A fit score above 60% for a mode does not mean you must build it — budget, terrain, and political feasibility still apply.

Starts deep negative (construction cost). Every year the system earns fare income, the line climbs. Where it crosses zero = break-even year. Positive at year 30 means the system created net economic value. Most public transit ends negative — that's why subsidies exist.

Staff wages, energy, maintenance, administration — every year, forever. When green revenue overtakes the red OpEx line, the system pays its own running costs. Until then, the gap is the annual subsidy the city must fund.

📐 How the risk score is calculated

Each risk is scored on two dimensions — both rated 1 to 5:

• Probability (P) — how likely is this risk to materialise?
1 = Rare · 2 = Unlikely · 3 = Possible · 4 = Likely · 5 = Almost certain

• Impact (I) — how serious would the consequences be?
1 = Negligible · 2 = Minor · 3 = Moderate · 4 = Serious · 5 = Severe

Score = P × I, giving a range of 1 to 25.

Why not 1–100? Using 1–5 scales forces evaluators to make meaningful distinctions without false precision. A 1–10 scale tends toward clustering at round numbers; 1–5 is the ISO 31000 standard for project risk assessment.

🔴 CRITICAL ≥ 16 (P×I: 4×4 or worse) 🟡 MEDIUM 9–15 🟢 LOW < 9
